1. Why RAG: models make up your policies

Take T-1008: Sofia asks for her $59.99 back on earbuds delivered two months ago. Checkpoint 02 looks the order up and checks the money, and it has no rule about dates. It has never read a policy, so nothing tells it that refunds close 14 days after delivery.

A model only "knows" the text it was trained on, frozen at a cutoff date. Nimbus's refund policy (v2.3, effective 2026-06-01) was never in it. Ask anyway and it predicts likely words: "30-day no-questions-asked refund" is a very likely phrase, because thousands of shops have one. Fluent text that isn't true is a hallucination.

A bright new hire on day one who never read the staff handbook, and who never says "I don't know".

Writing "only answer from company policy" in the prompt does nothing if the policy isn't in the prompt. The fix has to put the right policy text in front of the model, on every request.

RAG, Retrieval-Augmented Generation: before the model answers, your code looks up the relevant text and pastes it into the prompt. The model answers from that text and says where it read it.

Green is your code, violet is the model. The lookup is ordinary code: no model decides what to retrieve. The bootcamp's docs/architecture.md draws the same loop as Embed, Retrieve, Generate.

An open-book exam. You don't memorise the textbook. You find the right page, answer from it, and can point to the page.

Three ways to give a model your data

Good forWeak spot
RAG: look up the right pages for each questionFacts that change; answers that must name a sourceIf the lookup misses the page, the answer is wrong
Fine-tuning: train the model further on your examplesTone, format, one narrow skillBad at exact facts; stale the day the policy changes
Long context: paste every document into every promptA small set of documents that rarely changesThe one line that matters is lost in the pile, and the pile keeps growing

Deskhand already tried long context. Checkpoint 00's load_all_policies() in bot.py glues all six policy files into every prompt: about 2,300 tokens, under a tenth of a cent per ticket on gpt-4.1-mini. Cost isn't the problem. Accuracy is (v1 still made things up with every section in front of it), and so are sources (nobody can tell which section an answer used) and growth (six files today, thousands of pages at a real company). Fine-tuning changes how a model writes, not what it reliably knows: a fine-tuned bot still invents policy, in your house style.

2. Search by meaning: embeddings

Sofia's ticket never says "refund". She asks for her money back. Search the six policy files for "money back" and you get zero hits: Ctrl+F, SQL LIKE and a regex all compare characters. A list of synonyms never ends: "reimburse", "my cash", "return the funds", "chargeback". We need search that matches meaning, not spelling.

An embedding is a list of numbers that stands for the meaning of a text. Texts that mean similar things get similar lists. An embedding model turns any text, one word or a whole policy section, into a list of the same length. No person picks the numbers; they come out of training.

"Could I get my $59.99 back?"  ->  [0.021, -0.013, 0.044, …]   # 1,536 numbers; these values are made up

GPS coordinates for meaning: close coordinates, nearby places. Close lists, similar meaning.

A cartoon. Nobody designed these regions; they fall out of training. T-1008, the red diamond, lands among the refund phrases though it never says "refund". Where would "my parcel never came" land? In the blue group, sharing no word with "late delivery".

  • It's a separate model. Not the chat model: a much cheaper one that writes no text. Deskhand's default is OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small, 1,536 numbers per text. Anthropic has no embedding API, so Claude users still embed with OpenAI or Azure.
  • One embedding model for both sides. Embed the documents with one model and the queries with another, and the scores are meaningless: no error, just wrong results. Each model has its own map. Change the model, rebuild the index.

3. How close is close: cosine similarity

Treat each list of numbers as an arrow and ask how closely two arrows point the same way. That score is cosine similarity: 1 means the same direction (same meaning), 0 means at right angles.

Two people pointing at the sky. Both at the same star: 1. One points north, one east: 0. How far apart they stand doesn't matter, only the direction.

cosine(a, b) = (a1×b1 + a2×b2 + … + an×bn) / (length(a) × length(b))

# OpenAI's embeddings already have length 1, so it's just:
cosine(a, b) = a1×b1 + a2×b2 + … + an×bn      # multiply pairs, add up

By hand, with 3 numbers instead of 1,536

Pretend the model has three directions: about money, about delivery, something went wrong.

Textmoneydeliverywent wrong
"refund"0.80.00.6
"money back"0.60.00.8
"late delivery"0.00.80.6
refund · money back    = 0.8×0.6 + 0.0×0.0 + 0.6×0.8 = 0.48 + 0 + 0.48 = 0.96
refund · late delivery = 0.8×0.0 + 0.0×0.8 + 0.6×0.6 = 0    + 0 + 0.36 = 0.36

Every row has length 1 (0.8² + 0.6² = 0.64 + 0.36 = 1), so multiply-and-add is the whole calculation. The toy numbers were picked so the sums come out round; real vectors have 1,536 numbers nobody chose and nobody named.

What real scores look like

Measured on text-embedding-3-small: "refund" and "money back" score 0.60; "late delivery" and "my parcel never came" 0.47; "refund" and "my parcel never came" 0.25. None of the close pairs shares a word. Deskhand's 27 policy sections score between 0.05 and 0.69 against each other, median 0.36.

0.60 is not "60% similar", and real text rarely scores near 0. There is no universal "good" score. Read scores next to each other, within one query.

Distance or similarity? Deskhand's Qdrant reports cosine similarity: higher is closer. The bootcamp's Chroma reports a distance: lower is closer. Its default, l2, is the squared straight-line distance, and for vectors of length 1 that is exactly 2 − 2 × cosine. So a Chroma distance of 0.8 is a cosine of 0.6. Keep that in your pocket for checkpoint 5.

4. From numbers to search

A chunk is one piece of a document with its own embedding. The stored embeddings are the index: what every search reads. A vector store is the database that keeps each vector with its text and labels (metadata) and finds the nearest ones.

  1. Once (and again whenever a document changes): cut the documents into chunks, embed every chunk, store them.
  2. Per question: embed the question (one small call).
  3. Score it against the stored chunks.
  4. Hand the k closest to the model. Deskhand's k is 6 (TOP_K); the bootcamp's is 3.

In Deskhand's checkpoints/03-rag, which you open on Monday, a chunk is one ## section of a policy file: 27 sections from six files in shared/policies/. Building the index costs about 2,000 embedding tokens, about $0.00004. The store is Qdrant in local mode: a folder, qdrant_data/, no server.

Where similarity search goes wrong

Always answers

You get k results even when nothing is relevant. T-1035 asks about price matching, which the pack doesn't cover, and still gets six sections back.

Vague

"my order" is a little close to everything and very close to nothing.

Negation

"can never be refunded" lands near "can be refunded". One word barely moves the numbers.

Exact IDs

"status of O-48291": an order number has no meaning to match. The order API wins.

Retrieval ranks by meaning, not by authority. A refund-window question lands near refund-policy#window (14 days from delivery) and returns-and-exchanges#returns-window (30 days) alike, so both come back together. Which one applies is not a search question. Monday starts there.

Scale, for later: Deskhand checks every stored vector, 27 of them, which is instant. Ten million chunks of 1,536 numbers is about 61 GB of vectors per query, so large stores use an ANN index (approximate nearest neighbour, for example HNSW): like a B-tree for vectors, it may now and then miss the true closest one, in exchange for answering in milliseconds.

5. The bootcamp, checkpoint by checkpoint

rag-week-bootcamp builds a question-answering service over a fictional company's documents: Acme Corp's leave, expense, remote-work and onboarding policies, coding standards, an FAQ, messy meeting notes and a team directory CSV, ten files in data/knowledge/. It is FastAPI, ChromaDB and plain functions, with no LangChain on purpose (docs/architecture.md says why). Deskhand's 03-rag uses LangChain's QdrantVectorStore and MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter, so the bootcamp is the same pattern with the wrapping taken off. One LLM provider does both chat and embeddings, picked by LLM_PROVIDER: gemini or azure.

Each checkpoint is a complete copy of the app after that step, so you can read any one of them on its own. For each: the one idea, what to open, and where Deskhand does the same thing.

1 · session-01-baseline: grounding by hand

The idea
Grounding means putting the facts in the prompt. POST /ask answers from the model alone and invents a number of vacation days. POST /ask/grounded pastes a hard-coded leave-policy snippet into the prompt and says 20, and says it doesn't know about Git branching, which isn't in the snippet.
Open
docs/session-01.md, prompts/grounded_answer.py, routers/ask.py
In Deskhand
Checkpoint 00's load_all_policies(): the same idea with every policy pasted in and no selection.

2 · session-02-chunking: cut documents into pieces

The idea
You retrieve pieces, not files. chunk_document() cuts every 500 characters with a 50-character overlap, so a sentence cut at a boundary survives whole in a neighbour. chunk_by_sections() cuts before each #, ## or ### heading instead. loader.py turns .md, .csv, .pdf and .json into plain text first, so the chunker only sees text.
Open
docs/session-02.md (the two trade-off tables), rag/chunker.py, rag/loader.py, routers/ingest.py
In Deskhand
ingest.py cuts each policy file on ## only: 27 sections of about 75 tokens, one rule each, and no overlap needed because no section is ever cut.

The fixed chunker counts characters, not sentences, and the CSV loader joins every row into one text before it is cut. So a team-directory record can be split across two chunks. Wednesday: one parser per format, and a CSV row is one chunk.

3 · session-03-embeddings: vectors, a store, a search

The idea
embed_texts() turns every chunk into a vector in one batch. store.py upserts them into a Chroma collection with ids like leave-policy.md_0, and search() returns the closest chunks with a distance, lower is closer. GET /search tests retrieval with no chat-model call; POST /embed prints the distances between any texts you send.
Open
docs/session-03.md (the best of the eight for this weekend), rag/embedder.py, rag/store.py, routers/embed.py
In Deskhand
llm.embed_texts, metered so every call has a cost; Qdrant's policies collection with cosine; ids from uuid5("doc_id#section_id"), so re-running ingest.py replaces sections instead of duplicating them.

/embed prints the plain straight-line distance, while Chroma's search reports the squared one. The two endpoints' numbers are not on the same scale.

Ids are file name plus chunk number. Re-ingest with bigger chunks and the old higher-numbered chunks stay in the store, unless you pass fresh=true to wipe it. Wednesday's kb_sync.py re-indexes only the file that changed, and removes a deleted one.

4 · session-04-rag-pipeline: retrieve, inject, generate

The idea
The whole pattern. retrieve() embeds the question and returns the top 3 chunks; build_context() labels each one [Source: file]; the prompt says to answer from that context. prompt_style=minimal swaps in a bare prompt so you can watch the same chunks produce a worse answer.
Open
docs/session-04.md, rag/retriever.py, routers/query.py, prompts/rag_answer.py next to prompts/rag_answer_minimal.py
In Deskhand
retrieve_policy in nodes.py runs on every ticket before the planner. Its query is the triage category plus the ticket's first 500 characters (build_query in retrieve.py). The planner must cite doc_id#section_id, and rule 5 in rules.py rejects a refund or replacement that cites nothing, or cites a section that wasn't retrieved.

The response's sources are every chunk retrieved, whether the answer used them or not, and nothing checks the answer against them. In Deskhand a citation is checked in code.

5 · session-05-transparency: knowing when it doesn't know

The idea
Look at the best distance. At most 0.8 is "high", at most 1.4 "medium", above that "low"; above 1.6 the service says "I don't have information about that" without calling the model. GET /ui shows the answer with a confidence badge, its sources and their distances.
Open
docs/session-05.md, rag/retriever.py (score_confidence, is_no_result), config.py (the three thresholds); skim static/query.html
In Deskhand
No threshold at all. retrieve.py says to read the score "as a ranking within one query, not as a confidence", and T-1035 gets six sections whatever they score.

A fixed distance is a number for one embedding model and one corpus; the doc says so itself. With section 3's formula, 0.8, 1.4 and 1.6 are cosines of 0.6, 0.3 and 0.2. On Deskhand's model, the 27 policy sections score a median of 0.36 against each other, so "close enough" by distance says little about whether a chunk answers the question. In our run on 29 October (Azure embeddings), all seven in-domain eval questions came back "medium", none "high", and "Who is the CEO of Acme Corp?", which no document answers, scored 0.87: closer than the right leave-policy chunk for the vacation question, at 1.12. On Monday a model grades the chunks, enough or not, before anything is written.

6 · session-06-logging: why did it say that?

The idea
Every request gets an id (req_xxxxxxxx), and every step logs a line with it: the question, how many chunks, best and worst distance, the sources, the confidence. POST /compare runs the plain model and RAG side by side. The failure-modes table (out of domain, vague, subjective, temporal, cross-domain, too broad, near miss) is the part to keep.
Open
docs/session-06.md, main.py (logging.basicConfig), the logger.info lines in routers/query.py, routers/compare.py
In Deskhand
The request-id event log you know from checkpoint 02 (obslog.log_event). In 03-rag, retrieve_policy logs policy.retrieved with the section ids and the top score, so "what did the planner see?" is answered from the log alone.

The doc says the documents "don't track versions". The corpus disagrees: expense-policy-2024-update.md supersedes parts of expense-policy.md, and both are indexed. Remember it for Check 4.

7 · session-07-retrieval: what you search for, and where

The idea
rewrite=true sends the question to the model first, to rephrase it with the words the documents use ("What about time off?" becomes something like "employee vacation leave PTO policy"). source=leave-policy.md restricts the search to one file, as a filter inside Chroma's query. Read "When Rewriting Hurts" too: a precise question can get worse. In our run, the rewrite took "What about time off?" from a best distance of 1.12 to 0.84.
Open
docs/session-07.md, prompts/rewrite_query.py, rag/retriever.py (rewrite_query, retrieve), rag/store.py (search with where)
In Deskhand
build_query already adds the triage category: a rewrite with no model call. retrieve.py's doc_ids is a filter inside Qdrant's search, not rows dropped afterwards.

The rewrite runs on every request that asks for it, wanted or not, and costs a model call each time. The source filter is whatever the caller passes. On Monday a rewrite happens only after a grade says the chunks weren't enough, at most twice. On Wednesday the filter becomes access control: required, set from the caller's channel, never by the model.

8 · session-08-evaluation: test it, and guard it

The idea
eval/test_questions.json holds 12 cases: 5 factual, 2 vague, 2 out of domain, 3 injection. eval/run_eval.py sends each one to the running service and checks that the expected file was among the sources and the expected keywords are in the answer. Injection cases go to POST /safety/check, where a second prompt asks the model to answer SAFE or UNSAFE (LLM-as-judge).
Open
docs/session-08.md, eval/test_questions.json, eval/run_eval.py, prompts/safety_check.py, routers/safety.py
In Deskhand
T-1006's $10,000 "refund": the prompt asks the model to treat ticket text as data, and rules.py keeps the money in place whatever the model says. On Friday you meet the golden ticket set and the RAGAS evals in evals/rag_evals.py.

/query never calls /safety/check: the injection cases test the classifier on its own, not the service. A judge is one more model call that a clever enough message can talk round, which is why Deskhand's limits are if statements. And a keyword check can pass a wrong answer and fail a right one (Check 4).

7. Check yourself

Five questions. Decide your answer before you read the answers at the end.

Check 1: code, retrieval, or the order API?

  1. "Is order O-40021 still inside its refund window?"
  2. "The AeroFit earbuds won't pair after the update, what do I do?"
  3. "What's the status of O-48291?"
  4. Writing the reply that declines Sofia's refund, with the policy it rests on.

Check 2: by hand

With the three-number table in section 3: is "late delivery" closer to "refund" (0.36) or to "money back"? Work it out on paper.

Check 3: how many come back?

T-1035 asks about price matching. Nimbus's policy pack has no price-match policy. How many sections does Deskhand's retrieve() return? And what would the bootcamp's checkpoint 5 do with the same kind of question?

Check 4: the eval that grades the wrong thing

The bootcamp's eval has this case: "What is the maximum amount for meal reimbursement?", expected source expense-policy.md, expected keywords 50 and meal. Open data/knowledge/expense-policy-2024-update.md. What's wrong with the case? Then look at the first case, about vacation days, and leave-policy.md.

Check 5: two numbers, one meaning

The bootcamp calls a best distance of 0.8 "high confidence". In Deskhand, "refund" and "money back" score 0.60. Is a distance of 0.8 closer than a score of 0.60, further, or the same? Assume vectors of length 1.

Answers

  1. Code, retrieval, or the order API? 1 code: the window is a date sum from delivered_at, the same every run, and a search can't do arithmetic. 2 retrieval: no rule covers pairing trouble, and the answer lives in a help article. 3 the order API: an id has no meaning to match. 4 both: code has already decided "no"; retrieval supplies the wording and the citation. That's Monday's line.
  2. By hand. money back · late delivery = 0.6×0.0 + 0.0×0.8 + 0.8×0.6 = 0.48. Closer than "refund" (0.36), because "money back" carries more of the "went wrong" direction. A vector mixes topic, mood and urgency, so the nearest neighbour isn't always the one you'd file under the same topic. The real model said 0.34 and 0.32: a near tie, the other way round. Check real results; don't reason from the cartoon.
  3. How many come back? Six, always: TOP_K = 6, and similarity search ranks, it never filters. The recorded run put warranty#remedies first, pulled by the customer's side remark about a snapped clip. Cite that for a price match and the plan looks grounded and is wrong. The bootcamp says "I don't have information about that" only when the best distance crosses 1.6, a cosine of 0.2. In our run that caught "What is the capital of France?", but "Who is the CEO of Acme Corp?" scored 0.87 and went to the model; the model, not the threshold, said it didn't know. A threshold catches questions about another subject entirely, not questions about your subject that your documents don't answer, and T-1035 is the second kind.
  4. The eval that grades the wrong thing. The 2024 update raised team meals from $50 to $75 per person and the travel per diem from $60 to $75. The case expects the old file and the old number, so it would pass the stale answer and fails the current one. In our run on 29 October the service answered $75 from the update, and the case failed. Both files are in the index and nothing says which is current: that's Wednesday's manifest and status. The vacation case expects "15"; leave-policy.md says 20 days, the service said 20, and the case failed. A golden set is your judgement, and it can be wrong.
  5. Two numbers, one meaning. The same. Chroma's default distance is the squared straight-line distance, which for vectors of length 1 is 2 − 2 × cosine: 2 − 2 × 0.6 = 0.8. Distance down, similarity up, one geometry. Before you compare two systems' scores, find out what each one reports.

One exercise: catch it answering wrongly

Find one question the bootcamp answers wrongly. One we found: "What is the team meal limit?" with source=expense-policy.md answers $50, because the filter hides the 2024 update. Find your own. Ideas: anything the two expense files disagree on, anything from meeting-notes.md that says "next month" or "by friday" with no date, a near miss like parental leave for contractors. For your question write down:

  1. the question and the answer;
  2. the retrieved chunks: source file, distance, the first line of each;
  3. whether the chunk with the right answer was retrieved at all, and at which rank;
  4. so which part failed: retrieval (the right chunk wasn't there) or generation (it was there, and the answer is still wrong).
